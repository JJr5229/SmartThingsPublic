## PAGE lesson 01 | The answer was boring because the question was

Everyone has the same first disappointment. You type something reasonable, you get back something that reads like a form letter, and you conclude this has been oversold.

Look at what happened, though. You asked a question that could have come from anyone, about a situation you didn't describe, for a person you didn't mention, wanting an outcome you didn't state. You got the average of every possible answer. That is exactly what you asked for.

**This is good news.** It means the quality of what comes back is under your control, and the controls are all ordinary English.

---

## PAGE plain |

Here's the diagnosis, and you can run it in one glance. When an answer disappoints, one of four things was missing:

- **Who it's for.** It wrote for nobody in particular, so it wrote for everybody.
- **What you actually wanted.** You asked for help *with* something rather than asking for a specific thing.
- **What good looks like.** No length, no tone, no shape — so it guessed, and guessed long.
- **What only you know.** The circumstances that change the answer entirely.

The next four lessons are those four things, one each.

**Try this now.** Find an answer you were disappointed by. Work out which of the four was missing. It's usually obvious once you're looking for it.

## PAGE lesson 02 | Say who it's for

The largest single improvement available, and it costs about six words.

Writing changes completely depending on who's going to read it. You already know this — you don't text your sister the way you email your accountant. But when we type a request, we routinely leave the reader out, and then wonder why what comes back has no particular voice.

**Naming the reader gives it a target.** Not a description of the reader — the reader.

---

## PAGE plain |

```ask | Without a reader
Write a message explaining I'll be late.
```

```ask | With a reader
Write a message to my boss explaining I'll be an hour late. We get on well and she's relaxed, but she's chairing something at ten and needs to know now.
```

The second names who's reading, what they're like, and why the timing matters. What comes back is short, warm and useful. The first is a form letter.

**Try this now.** Take any request you'd normally write and add six words naming the reader. Nothing else. Compare.

## PAGE lesson 03 | Say what you actually want

The second most common gap, and the easiest to fix once you notice it.

Most people ask for *help with* something. "Help me with an email." "Can you help with my CV." It's polite, and it's how we'd open with a person — but a person would then ask what kind of help. Here, you'll simply get whatever seemed most likely.

**Ask for the thing, not for help with the thing.**

---

## PAGE plain |

Not *help me with this letter* but **write**, **shorten**, **explain**, **compare**, **check**, **list**, **rewrite**, **summarise**, **plan**, **quiz me**.

```ask | Vague verb
Can you help me with this contract?
```

```ask | Specific verb
Explain what this contract obliges me to do, in plain English, and list anything that looks unusual.
```

The second one has two clear jobs. You can tell whether it did them. The first can't be judged at all.

**Try this now.** Rewrite a request so it starts with a verb.

## PAGE lesson 04 | Say what good looks like

You know what a good answer looks like. It doesn't. Words like *good*, *professional*, *engaging* and *nice* carry almost nothing — ask ten people what "professional" means and you'll get ten answers.

**Replace the judgement word with what it would actually take to satisfy you.** Usually that's three things: how long, what tone, what shape.

---

## PAGE plain |

- **How long.** *Under a hundred words. Three sentences. One page.* This alone fixes most disappointment, because the default is long.
- **What tone.** *Warm but brief. Formal. Like I'm texting a friend. Plain, no enthusiasm.*
- **What shape.** *A numbered list. A table comparing them. Only the subject lines. Three options, no explanation.*

```callout | The strongest move in the whole book
Show it an example. Paste in something you wrote and liked, and say *match this voice.* One real example teaches it more about how you sound than three paragraphs of description ever will.
```

**Try this now.** Add a length and a tone to a request you've already made. Watch it tighten.

## PAGE lesson 05 | Tell it the things only you know

It knows nothing about your life. Not your job, your family, your town, your history with the person you're writing to, or what happened last Tuesday. Not because it's being careful — it genuinely has no idea, every time you begin.

Everything that makes your situation *yours* has to come from you. That's not a limitation to work around. It's the whole job.

---

## PAGE plain |

The useful part is knowing what's relevant, because "more context" doesn't mean everything.

- **The relationship.** Close, distant, awkward, professional, strained.
- **The history.** What's already happened, what's been tried, what was said last time.
- **The constraint.** Money, timing, a policy, something you can't say.
- **The outcome.** What you want to be true afterwards.

```callout | Write it once
Most of your context repeats. Keep four or five sentences about yourself in a note on your phone — what you do, who you deal with, how you like to sound. Paste it in when it's relevant. Every answer improves at once.
```

**Try this now.** Write those five sentences. It's the highest-return ten minutes in this book.

## PAGE lesson 06 | Ask for three, not one

A quiet trick that removes most of the pressure.

When you ask for one thing, you have to have described it correctly, because you're going to get exactly one attempt at what you asked for. When you ask for three, you get a range — and choosing between three real options is far easier than imagining the right one in advance.

**Three versions also tells you something.** If all three miss in the same direction, your request was the problem. If one is close, you've found your starting point.

---

## PAGE plain |

```ask | One shot
Write a thank-you note for a wedding present.
```

```ask | Three to choose from
Give me three versions of a thank-you note for a wedding present — one warm and chatty, one short and sincere, one slightly funny. My aunt gave us a set of knives we genuinely like.
```

You'll pick one in four seconds, and often take a line from another.

**Try this now.** Ask for three of something, deliberately different from each other.

## PAGE lesson 07 | Nudge it, don't start over

The biggest time-waster is throwing away a near-miss and rewriting the whole request. Don't. The conversation is still open, and it remembers everything you've said.

Two or three small corrections beat one perfect opening request, almost every time. Each takes about four seconds.

```callout | The phrases that do the most work
Shorter — half that. · Longer, with a specific example. · Warmer. · More formal. · That's too salesy. · Keep the second paragraph, redo the rest. · Give me three versions. · Now make it an email. · Say that like I've never done this before. · That's not quite what I meant — I actually need…
```

---

## PAGE plain |

It's the difference between briefing a photographer and firing one. You don't start again after the first shot. You say *good — now step left, and try it brighter.*

**You:** *Write a note to my neighbour about their tree overhanging my garden.*
**It:** a polite, stiff, four-paragraph letter.
**You:** *Too formal. We're friendly, we chat over the fence.*
**It:** warmer, shorter, more natural.
**You:** *Good. Cut the last line, it's passive-aggressive.*
**It:** the version you send.

Forty seconds, three nudges. You'd have spent longer staring at a blank page.

**Try this now.** Take anything and nudge it three times without rewriting your original request.

## PAGE lesson 08 | Let it ask you

The move for when you don't yet know what you want — which is more often than anyone admits.

Sometimes the reason a request comes out vague is that the thing in your head is vague. No amount of careful phrasing fixes that, because there's nothing precise to phrase yet.

So hand the problem back.

```ask | When you can't articulate it
I'm trying to sort something out but I can't describe it clearly yet. Ask me questions until we've worked out what I actually want, then help.
```

---

## PAGE plain |

This works because answering a specific question is far easier than composing a description from nothing. You'll say things in response to a question that you'd never have thought to write down.

Two more worth keeping:

```ask | For a decision
Ask me five questions that would help me think this through, then wait for my answers before saying anything.
```

```ask | For something you're avoiding
I've been putting this off for two weeks. Ask me what's actually stopping me.
```

**Try this now.** Use the first one on something genuinely unresolved. Notice that you end up doing the thinking — which is the point.

## PAGE card | The Ask Builder

Not a template. Four questions, answered on paper. **Your four answers, read out in order, are the message.**

- **1 — Who is this for?** Name them and say what they're like. *My landlord, who I get on with but who ignores emails.*
- **2 — What do you want to happen?** State the outcome, not the task. *I want the heating fixed this week without souring things.*
- **3 — How long, and what tone?** *Under a hundred words. Polite but clearly not a first request.*
- **4 — What do you know that it doesn't?** The facts only you have. *It's been five days, I've messaged twice, there's a toddler in the house.*

---

## PAGE plain |

Read those four answers straight through and you have written this:

```ask | The four answers, in order
This is for my landlord, who I get on with but who ignores emails. I want the heating fixed this week without souring things. Keep it under a hundred words, polite but clearly not a first request. It's been five days, I've messaged twice, and there's a toddler in the house.
```

Nobody had to fill in a blank or learn a format. You answered four questions about your own life and the message assembled itself.

```callout | Why paper
Answering out loud or on paper gets you past the blank-box freeze. The box asks you to compose. The four questions only ask you to answer.
```

**Try this now.** Run the builder on something real. Keep the page.

## PAGE plain | Twelve before-and-afters

The rest of this book is worked examples. Each shows a thin request, the same request done properly, and one line on what changed. Read them or skim them — they're here to be recognised, not studied.

```asks | Before
Write an email to my son's teacher.
```

```asks | After
Write an email to my son's Year 4 teacher. He's been coming home upset about maths and says he's the only one who doesn't get it. I'd like to ask what she's seeing in class and whether we should be doing something at home. Warm, not accusing, under 150 words.
```

**What changed:** who, what's happened, what I want, and the tone to avoid.


```asks | After

```

**What changed:** the history, and the specific outcome I'm asking for.

## PAGE plain | When it still doesn't work

**"I gave it loads of context and it's still generic."** Check you gave *relevant* context rather than lots of it. Four sentences about the relationship beat two paragraphs about your week.

**"It keeps writing more than I want."** Length is the instruction people most often forget. Say the number. It obeys.

**"It sounds like it, not like me."** Stop describing your voice and show it. Paste in something you wrote.

**"I don't know what I want."** Lesson 8. Let it ask you.

**"It ignored part of what I asked."** Long requests with several jobs get partly answered. Number them, or do one at a time.

## PAGE plain | If you only do one thing

Take one habit and leave the other seven.

**Name the reader.**

Six words, at the front of any request that produces writing. *Write a message to my landlord, who…* *An email to my son's teacher, who…* *A note to my neighbour, who…*

It's the smallest change in this book and the largest effect. It gives the answer a target, a tone and a reason to exist, and it takes less effort than reading this page did.

Everything else here is refinement on top of that one move.
