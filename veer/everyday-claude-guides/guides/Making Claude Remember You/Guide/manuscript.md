## PAGE lesson 01 | Why you keep repeating yourself

If you've used this for a few weeks you'll have noticed the tax. Every new conversation begins with the same three sentences. *I run a small gardening business. I'm writing to a customer. I don't like anything too formal.*

Type that four hundred times a year and it becomes the reason you stop bothering for small things.

**The fix is to say it once and have it apply from then on.** That's the whole subject of this book.

---

## PAGE plain |

Two separate ideas, and it's worth keeping them apart:

- **Who you are and what your situation is.** Facts that don't change week to week.
- **How you want it to behave.** Length, tone, what to always do, what never to do.

The first improves the *content* of answers. The second improves the *form*. Most people set neither, then wonder why every answer needs the same three corrections.

```callout | The test for whether this is worth doing
Have you typed the same background sentence more than three times? Then it belongs somewhere permanent.
```

**Try this now.** Think back over your last few conversations. What did you explain more than once?

## PAGE lesson 02 | Writing down who you are

The highest-value ten minutes in this series, and it's a piece of writing rather than a setting.

**Five or six sentences.** Enough that a capable stranger could write on your behalf; not so much that the important parts get buried.

What earns its place:

- **What you do**, in a sentence. Work, or how you spend your time.
- **Who's around you** if it's relevant. Family, customers, the people you write to.
- **How you sound.** Warm and brief. Plain, no exclamation marks. Dry.
- **What you're usually trying to do.** Understand things, write things, plan things.
- **Anything that changes every answer.** Where you live, if that matters. A constraint you always have.

---

## PAGE plain |

```asks | If writing it feels awkward
Ask me questions about myself, my situation and how I like things written, until you have enough to write a short "about me" paragraph I can reuse. Then write it in the first person.
```

Answering questions is easier than composing from nothing, and it usually produces something better than you'd have written cold.

```warn | Keep it to what's useful, not what's private
This is a description of your situation, not your file. Your health, your finances, other people's private details — leave them out and mention them only in the specific conversation where they matter. See Is This Safe?
```

**Try this now.** Write the six sentences, or have it interview you. Keep them somewhere you can copy from.

## PAGE lesson 03 | Standing rules for how you like things

The second half. Not who you are — how you want it to behave.

Most people's rules are the corrections they keep having to make. Notice what you type twice a week and turn it into a rule.

**Common ones worth having:**

- *Keep answers short unless I ask for detail.*
- *Don't open with a summary of my own question.*
- *No exclamation marks, and don't tell me a question is a great question.*
- *If you're unsure, say so rather than guessing.*
- *When I ask for writing, give me the writing without an explanation of it.*

---

## PAGE plain |

**Write rules as behaviour, not personality.** "Be concise" is vague. "Answers under 150 words unless I ask for more" is a rule it can follow.

**Add them as you notice them.** The good set is built over a month of noticing your own corrections, not written in one sitting.

```callout | The one most people want first
"Don't be effusive. No praise, no enthusiasm about the task, no offering three follow-up suggestions I didn't ask for." Whether that's an improvement is a matter of taste — but a lot of people find it is, and never realise they can ask.
```

**Try this now.** Write down the three corrections you make most often. Those are your first three rules.

## PAGE lesson 04 | A workspace for one part of your life

The next step up. Rather than one setup for everything, keep a separate space for a recurring situation, with its own background and its own rules.

**Worth having its own space:**

- A hobby or a subject you're learning, where the vocabulary and your level are constant.
- A recurring responsibility — a committee, a club, caring for a relative.
- Work, if you'd rather keep it apart from everything else.
- A long project, where every conversation needs the same background.

---

## PAGE plain |

**Set the background at the level of the situation, not the task.** *This is for helping me care for my mother, who is 84 and lives alone* — then every conversation in that space starts from there.

**The benefit is cumulative.** Conversation twenty starts as well-informed as conversation one ended, without you doing anything.

```callout | Don't over-organise
Two or three spaces for things you genuinely return to. Not fifteen. A structure you have to maintain is a structure you'll abandon, and everything here still works from an ordinary conversation.
```

**Try this now.** Name the one part of your life you'd most like it to already understand.

## PAGE lesson 05 | Giving it your own reference material

The step that changes the quality most, and the one fewest people take.

Alongside a description, you can give a workspace **documents** — the actual material it should work from. Then it answers from your facts rather than general knowledge.

**What's worth adding:**

- **Things you've written that you liked.** The single best way to teach it your voice.
- **Reference facts you keep repeating.** Prices, dates, names, the way you do a recurring thing.
- **A document you keep returning to.** A policy, a plan, a set of rules, a manual.

---

## PAGE plain |

**Two or three good documents beat twenty.** More material is not better material; it's more to search and more to get confused between.

**Say what each one is.** *This is our club's constitution* and *this is last year's programme* tells it how to use them, which a filename doesn't.

```warn | Only what you're comfortable storing
Anything you add sits there until you remove it. Apply the rule from Is This Safe?: nothing you'd mind a stranger reading, and nothing that belongs to someone else.
```

**Try this now.** Find two things you've written that sound like you. Those are the most valuable documents you own for this purpose.

## PAGE lesson 06 | When to keep things separate

Setting things up once has one failure mode: context leaking where it isn't wanted.

**Keep separate:** anything genuinely private from anything you might show someone. Work from personal, if a colleague ever looks over your shoulder. A sensitive situation — a health matter, a family difficulty, money trouble — from the space you use for ordinary things.

**Use a plain conversation with no setup when** the situation is one-off, when your standing rules would get in the way, or when you'd rather it *didn't* know your usual context.

---

## PAGE plain |

That last one is worth saying plainly. Sometimes you want a general answer, not one shaped by everything it knows about you — a fresh view on a decision, or a question where your own framing is the problem.

```callout | The check before you set anything up
Ask: would I mind if this background appeared in an answer while someone was looking at my screen? If yes, it belongs in its own space, or in no space at all.
```

**Try this now.** Look at what you were about to write into a permanent setting. Decide whether it belongs there or in one conversation.

## PAGE lesson 07 | Keeping it current

The quiet failure. You set this up beautifully in March, and by November it describes a job you've left, a project that finished, and a tone you've gone off.

Stale context is worse than none, because it confidently shapes answers around a life you no longer have.

**Read it through twice a year.** Ten minutes. Delete what's finished, update what's changed, cut the rules you no longer notice.

---

## PAGE plain |

**Delete more than you add.** These accumulate. A description that's grown to two pages has stopped being a summary of you.

```asks | The review
Here's the description I set up some time ago. Ask me questions about what's changed, then give me a shorter updated version.
```

**And if answers start feeling oddly off**, check the setup before you blame the tool. A rule you set in a mood six months ago is a common cause and an easy fix.

**Try this now.** Put a reminder in your calendar for six months' time that says "check the about-me."

## PAGE card | Your "about me" page

Write these six sentences once. Keep them where you can copy them — a note on your phone works. Paste them in wherever they're relevant, or put them into a permanent setting.

- **What I do.** One sentence about work, or how you spend your time.
- **Who's around me.** Family, customers, colleagues — only if it affects what you'd ask for.
- **How I sound.** Three adjectives, or one sentence. Warm and brief. Plain, no enthusiasm. Dry.
- **What I usually want.** Understanding things, drafting things, planning things, being quizzed.
- **The thing that changes every answer.** Where I live, a constant constraint, a standing preference.
- **My three standing rules.** The three corrections you make most often, written as instructions.

**Leave out:** health, finances, anything about other people, and anything you'd mind a stranger reading.

## PAGE plain | If you only do one thing

Take one habit and leave the other six.

**Write the six sentences.**

Not the workspaces, not the documents, not the standing rules — a short description of who you are, how you sound, and what you're usually trying to do, kept where you can copy from it.

Even if you never touch a single setting, pasting those six sentences at the top of a conversation makes the answer better than any technique in the rest of this series. Everything else here is a way of not having to paste them.

Ten minutes, once. It's the best trade in these books.
