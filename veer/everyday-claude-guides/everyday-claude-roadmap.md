# The Everyday Claude Guides — Product Roadmap & Framework
*Planning doc for the consumer line. Nothing here is built yet. Sibling to `claude-guides-roadmap.md`, which governs the small-business line.*

Last updated: 2026-09-02

---

## 0. Why this is a separate line, not a beginner shelf inside the business line

The small-business line already has beginner titles — but they are beginner *for a business owner*. Every promise in that line resolves to hours saved or money made, and the reader is assumed to have a reason to be there: they run something, and it's costing them time.

This line has no such reader. The person here has heard about AI for two years, feels vaguely behind, has maybe opened a chatbot once and typed "hello," got a wall of text back, and closed the tab. They don't have a workflow to optimize. They have a Tuesday.

That changes almost every decision — the promise, the price, the page density, the channel, even the physical format. Which is why this is its own product line with its own economics, sharing only the production system and the Veer identity.

**Positioning line:** *"The Everyday Claude Guides — start where you are."*

**Not** "learn AI." **Not** "unlock productivity." The promise is that a specific ordinary thing gets easier, and that the reader stops feeling stupid about a technology everyone keeps talking about.

## 1. The decisions (locked)

| Decision | Choice | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Audience** | Regular adults with little-to-no AI experience and, often, modest tech confidence. Retirees, parents, caregivers, students, hobbyists, career-changers. | Genuinely underserved. Every AI guide on the market is written for someone already leaning in. |
| **Line name** | **The Everyday Claude Guides** | Reads as a collected series, like a publisher's imprint. Sits under Veer alongside "Claude for Small Business" without pretending to be its twin. |
| **Format** | Short, low-density PDFs ($5–9 each) + bundles + a **printed/large-print edition** as the differentiator | Consumers won't pay small-business prices for a PDF about a free app. Volume and charm carry this line, not depth. |
| **Primary channel** | **Etsy first**, own site second — the inverse of the business line | Consumers browse Etsy. Small-business owners don't. This is the single biggest strategic difference between the two lines. |
| **Maintenance** | Same model: evergreen core + one quarantined "Current as of [quarter]" section | Already proven on the business line. Don't re-invent it. |
| **Relationship to the business line** | Top of funnel, one direction only | A hobbyist who starts selling becomes a small-business buyer. Never cross-sell downward. |

## 2. Who this reader actually is (write to this person, every page)

Not a persona sketch for a pitch deck — a working spec. Every draft gets checked against it.

- **They are not stupid.** They are uninitiated. Those are completely different, and most beginner AI content confuses them. Never explain *why* something is useful in a tone that implies they wouldn't have guessed.
- **They are slightly embarrassed.** They feel late. A meaningful part of this product's value is removing that feeling in the first two pages, before any instruction happens.
- **They have been burned by tech writing before.** "Simply navigate to settings" is not simple if you don't know what settings are. Assume nothing is obvious.
- **They will quit at the first unexplained word.** One unexplained term — *prompt*, *model*, *context* — and the guide is closed. Jargon is not a style problem here; it's a churn problem.
- **They want one win, fast.** Not a tour of the features. One thing that works, that they can show someone.
- **Some of them are reading this on paper, at a kitchen table, in reading glasses.** Design for that reader and the screen reader is fine too. The reverse is not true.

### Hard tone rules for this line

1. **No unexplained jargon, ever.** If a term is needed, it gets defined in the same sentence, in ordinary words, the first time it appears — even if the glossary already covers it. Repetition is cheaper than a lost reader.
2. **No acronym frameworks.** The business line's R·T·C·F·C works because its reader likes systems. This reader does not want to memorize five letters. Teach the same substance as plain habits: *say who you are, say what you want, say what "good" looks like.*
3. **Describe the goal, then the button.** "You're looking for the box where you type — it's the big empty one in the middle" beats any instruction that names a UI element the reader can't find.
4. **Never say "just."** "Just click," "just type," "it's just." That word does more damage to a nervous reader than any other word in tech writing.
5. **Show the mess.** Real examples with real awkwardness — the passive-aggressive email to a neighbor, the confusing insurance letter. Polished demo examples read as fake to this audience.
6. **One idea per page.** Low density is a feature, not padding. Pages are cheap; a lost reader isn't.
7. **Every guide ends with permission to stop.** "If you only ever do the thing on page 4, this was worth it." Reduces the abandonment guilt that kills repeat purchases.

## 3. The catalog

Build in this order. Each is a standalone sellable PDF; together they ladder up in price and commitment.

### Tier 0 — Free (email capture)

- **"Your First Ten Minutes With Claude"** — 4–6 pages, enormous type, one path, one win. Not a tour. The reader opens the app, types one specific thing, and gets one specific good result. This is the top of the entire funnel and probably the most important single asset in the line.
- **"The No-Jargon Claude Glossary"** — 15 words, not the business line's 20. **Deliberately drops** MCP, Skills, Claude Code, tokens, and API. Keeps: prompt, chat, model, context, project, artifact, connector, plan tiers, hallucination, and friends. A distinct product from the SMB glossary — do not just re-skin it, the omissions are the point.

### Tier 1 — Starting out — $5–7 each *(the Etsy engine)*

1. **Claude for Absolute Beginners** — what it is, getting an account, the interface, your first real conversation, what to do when it goes weird. The anchor beginner title.
2. **Asking Better Questions** — consumer prompting, taught as habits rather than a framework. Before/after pairs from ordinary life, not marketing.
3. **101 Everyday Things to Ask Claude** — pure browse-and-use utility. Highest impulse-buy potential in the line and the strongest Etsy listing. Likely the volume leader.
4. **Is This Safe? Privacy, Trust, and Knowing When to Double-Check** — the objection guide. What Claude does and doesn't see, what it gets wrong and why, how to sanity-check an answer, and the three categories where you always confirm with a human. **Strong candidate to move up the build order** — for this audience it's a trust product as much as an instruction product, and it makes every other guide in the line safer to sell.

### Tier 2 — In your actual life — $9–12 each

5. **Letters, Emails, and Awkward Messages** — the complaint, the condolence, the boundary with a relative, the note to the landlord, the "I have to say no." Probably the highest emotional-value title in the catalog.
6. **Around the House** — meal planning from what's in the fridge, shopping lists, repairs, decluttering, "what is this thing and how do I fix it."
7. **Family Life** — homework help that teaches instead of cheats (handle the ethics head-on, don't dodge it), trip planning, caregiving logistics, the hard conversations.
8. **Paperwork and Plain English** — translating an insurance letter, a lease, a warranty, a bill, a form. **Heavy human-in-the-loop framing throughout; explicitly not legal or financial advice.**
9. **Learning Something New** — a language, an instrument, a hobby, a certification. Claude as an infinitely patient tutor who never sighs.

### Tier 3 — Going a bit further — $12–15 each

10. **Photos, Files, and Documents** — uploading things and asking about them. First guide where the reader does something beyond typing.
11. **Making Claude Remember You** — the consumer cut of Projects and custom instructions, framed as "so you stop re-explaining yourself" rather than as a workspace feature.
12. **Claude on Your Phone** — voice, on-the-go use, the app. Genuinely different ergonomics; earns its own guide.

### Explicitly excluded from this line

MCP, the API, Claude Code, hooks, automations, integrations that require setting up a second service, and anything with a developer concept underneath it. If a title needs one of those to work, it belongs in the business line or nowhere. **This exclusion is the line's spine — protect it.** The temptation to add "one more advanced one" is exactly how a beginner line stops being a beginner line.

### One title to think hard about before building

**Health questions.** Real demand, real value, real risk. If it ships, it ships narrowly — *preparing for an appointment, understanding what the doctor already told you, writing down your questions beforehand* — and never as symptom-checking or anything resembling diagnosis. **Recommendation: defer past the first two phases**, and if built, get the framing right before writing a single page. The liability and trust cost of getting this wrong outweighs the revenue.

### Bundles & editions

- **"The Everyday Claude Collection"** — everything, $29–39. The anchor.
- **Large-Print Edition** — the same guides, reset at ~16pt with more white space. Costs one build variable to produce and serves a real, largely ignored audience. Sell at a small premium or as an accessibility option at the same price; either way it's a genuine reason to choose Veer.
- **Printed spiral-bound booklet** — Veer is a Design · Print · Tech studio, so this is a capability nobody else in this category has. A physical booklet that sits next to the laptop is a *better product* for this reader than a PDF, not just a different one. Highest-margin item in the line and the hardest to copy. **Flagged as the strategic bet worth testing early.**
- **"What's New" update pack** — same quarterly model as the business line, shared production cycle.

## 4. Pricing & funnel math

- Free "First Ten Minutes" → email list.
- Tripwire: $5–7 Tier 1 single, usually *101 Everyday Things* or *Absolute Beginners*.
- Core offer: the Collection at $29–39.
- Physical booklet: the upsell that pays for the line's margin.

Rule of thumb carried over from the business line: singles priced so the bundle is a no-brainer (bundle ≈ 40–50% of buying all singles separately).

**Deliberate difference from the business line:** volume over margin. These are ~$6 products sold to a large, browsing audience, not ~$25 products sold to a qualified one. That means more titles, shorter each, and Etsy SEO doing the work that a sales page does on the business side.

## 5. Channel strategy

| Channel | Role | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Etsy** | **Primary.** The traffic engine and the discovery surface. | Consumers actually shop here for printables and guides. ⚠️ Same standing caveat as the business line: Etsy is inconsistent about AI info-products and has removed digital listings before. Keep master files off-platform, funnel to the Veer list, and don't build the line's survival on a single Etsy shop. |
| **Own Veer guides site + Payhip** | Bundle sales, email capture, the Collection | Same checkout plumbing already decided for the business line. No new infrastructure. |
| **Pinterest** | Underrated for this audience specifically | This reader searches Pinterest. Vertical 2:3 pins per title, pointing at the free magnet. Cheap, compounding, and better matched to this line than to the business one. |
| **Print (direct / local / Etsy physical listing)** | The differentiator | Physical booklets also sidestep the digital-AI-listing policy risk entirely, which is a quiet second reason to pursue them. |

**Etsy risk is materially higher for this line than the business line** — not because the policy differs, but because Etsy is the primary channel rather than a supplementary one. Mitigation: own-site listings live from day one, email capture on every free download, and the printed edition as a non-digital fallback.

## 6. Production roadmap

**Phase 0 — Prep.** Decide the design deviations in §7. Build the free magnet first and use it to lock the look, exactly as the business line locked its template on its first real guide.

**Phase 1 — MVP.** Free magnet + glossary + **three Tier 1 guides** (*Absolute Beginners*, *101 Everyday Things*, *Is This Safe?*) + a "Starter Bundle." List all three on Etsy. This is enough to test whether the consumer audience converts at all before committing to twelve titles.

**Phase 2 — Depth.** Tier 2 life-area guides. These are where the line becomes something people recommend to their mother, which is the actual growth mechanism here.

**Phase 3 — The physical bet.** Print the Collection as a spiral-bound booklet and the large-print edition. Test locally before scaling.

**Phase 4 — Recurring.** Fold this line into the same quarterly "What's New" cycle as the business line. One update pass, two product lines.

## 7. Production system — what changes from the business line

The `claude-guide-builder` skill already handles the whole pipeline. **Recommendation: extend it with an audience switch rather than fork a second skill** — the phases, the PDF build, the disclaimer discipline, and the evergreen/update-pack split are all identical, and two near-duplicate skills will drift apart within a quarter.

What the switch changes:

| | Small Business line | Everyday line |
|---|---|---|
| **Body type** | Standard print size | **Larger — ~12–13pt baseline, 16pt in the large-print edition** |
| **Page density** | Dense, several ideas per page | **One idea per page.** More pages, fewer words on each |
| **Cover** | Ember gradient, cinematic, dark | Same ember gradient — **the identity is shared and stays shared** |
| **Signature artifact** | Prompt pack, decision table, automation recipe | Almost always a **plain-language ask list** — real sentences the reader types verbatim, no `[brackets]` to fill in |
| **Jargon budget** | Terms defined once, then used | **Zero.** Defined at every appearance |
| **Screenshots** | Optional, quarantined | **More valuable here** — but still quarantined, still captioned with a date, still never the only way a step is conveyed |
| **Price band** | $9–29 | $5–15 |
| **Etsy eligibility** | Foundations tier only | **Everything** — no tier in this line carries the automation-guide policy risk |

The `[bracketed]` fill-in-the-blank convention is the one production habit that must **not** carry over. It's efficient for a business reader building a prompt library and genuinely confusing for someone who has never written a prompt. Everyday-line artifacts are written as complete, typeable sentences.

## 8. Legal & trust

Same independent-educator disclaimer, verbatim, on every product — guide final page, sales fine print, every listing. No Anthropic or Claude logos or marks. Same non-negotiable status as the business line.

**Two additions specific to this line**, because the subject matter reaches further into people's lives:

- **A standing "when to involve a human" rule.** Any guide touching money, law, health, or a decision with real consequences carries an explicit line saying Claude helps you *understand and prepare*, and the decision stays with a qualified person. Not fine print — visible, in the body, where the reader will actually see it.
- **Honesty about being wrong.** This audience has not internalized that a confident answer can be false. Every guide in the line says so plainly at least once. It costs a paragraph and it's the difference between a reader who trusts the guides and one who gets burned and blames Veer.
