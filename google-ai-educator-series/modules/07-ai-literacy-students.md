# Module 07 — Teaching Students With and About AI

**Time:** 90 minutes
**Prerequisite:** Modules 01–06
**Artifact produced:** A redesigned AI-resilient assessment

---

## Why this module exists

Modules 01–06 made staff more effective. This one is about students, and it is the
module school leaders care most about — because it addresses the question they are
actually being asked by families and by teachers: *what do we do about students using AI?*

The framing matters. Common Sense Media's 2026 research found **70% of teens use AI for
schoolwork.** The question is not whether students will use AI. They already do. The
question is whether the adults have a coherent response beyond detection and punishment.

SPPS is already well positioned here — the district runs CIPA-compliant digital
citizenship lessons each fall, embeds digital citizenship in content-area lessons and
PBIS restorative practices, and points families to Google's AI Literacy Guide for Teens
and Common Sense Media. **This module plugs into that existing structure rather than
proposing a parallel one.**

## Objectives

By the end, participants can:

1. Distinguish teaching *with* AI from teaching *about* AI, and do both
2. Redesign an assessment so it measures learning in a world where AI exists
3. Set and communicate clear, defensible classroom AI expectations
4. Connect AI literacy to the district's existing digital citizenship cycle
5. Handle a suspected-AI-use situation without a detection tool

## What you need enabled ⚠️

- **Gemini in Classroom is 18+ and English-only** — it is a *teacher* tool, not a student
  tool. Student-facing AI depends on whether SPPS has enabled Gemini for under-18
  accounts. **Confirm this before delivery**; it determines whether the hands-on block
  designs student-facing activities or adult-mediated ones.
- Note that Gemini in Classroom has been expanding to users of all ages with
  contextualized starter prompts for students — availability moves quickly. Re-verify.

## Facilitator script

### 1. Reframe the problem (15 min)

Open with the number: **70% of teens use AI for schoolwork.**

Then ask the room what their current response is. Most will describe some mix of
prohibition, suspicion, and detection.

**Address detection directly, because staff are relying on it.** AI detection tools are
unreliable. They produce false positives, and — this is the part that matters most —
**they produce false positives disproportionately on the writing of English language
learners and students who write in a plainer register.** In a district with SPPS's
demographics, a detection-first response is not merely ineffective, it is inequitable.
An accusation based on a detector score is not defensible to a family.

That lands hard, and it should. Then give the room somewhere to go:

> If you cannot detect it, and you cannot prevent it, the remaining move is to **design
> for it** — assessments where AI use is either visible, irrelevant, or explicitly part
> of the work.

### 2. Teaching *about* AI (20 min)

Connect to what SPPS already does. Digital citizenship lessons run every fall; AI
literacy belongs inside that cycle, not beside it.

Core concepts students need, matched to the district's existing resources:

- **What generative AI actually is** — pattern prediction, not understanding, not a
  search engine, not a source of truth
- **Why it is confidently wrong** — hallucination, and why fluent text feels credible
- **Bias** — where training data bias shows up in output
- **Data** — what you give away when you type into a consumer tool
- **Where it helps and where it robs you** — using AI to get past a blank page versus
  using it instead of thinking

Point participants to resources already endorsed by the district: **Google's AI Literacy
Guide for Teens**, **Common Sense Media's** AI resources and district toolkit, and their
digital literacy curriculum with 20-minute AI lessons for grades K–12.

**The most useful classroom activity:** have students prompt an AI about something they
personally know well — a hobby, their neighborhood, their home language, their culture —
and find the errors. Nothing teaches skepticism faster than watching a confident machine
be wrong about *your* life. It also flips the expertise dynamic in a way that works
especially well with students who are not usually positioned as the experts in the room.

### 3. Assessment redesign (25 min)

The practical heart of the module. Four strategies — teach all four, since different
subjects need different ones:

**Make the process visible.** Assess drafts, revisions, conferences, and process
artifacts, not just the final product. A finished essay reveals little; a revision
history reveals learning. This is the single most robust strategy.

**Move up the thinking.** AI does summarizing and explaining well. It does *this text
against your community's experience*, *this data from our own class experiment*, and
*your reasoning about a local decision* poorly. Anchor tasks in local, personal, and
current context that no model has.

**Bring it in the room.** Discussion, presentation, defense, performance, live problem
solving. Not a replacement for written work — a complement that makes the written work
verifiable.

**Make AI use explicit and assessable.** Assign the AI use, then assess the judgment:
have students generate with AI, then critique, fact-check, and improve the output.
Assess the critique. This teaches exactly the skill they will need, and it is impossible
to shortcut with the tool itself.

Then teach the **traffic-light expectation model** — students are confused because rules
differ by teacher and often go unstated:

- 🔴 **Red — no AI.** This task measures something you must be able to do unaided.
- 🟡 **Yellow — AI for specified parts.** Brainstorming yes, drafting no. Cite what you
  used.
- 🟢 **Green — AI encouraged.** Show your prompts and what you changed.

The rule: **state the color on every assignment.** Most academic-integrity conflict comes
from unstated expectations, not from defiance.

### 4. When you suspect AI use (10 min)

Practical and important. Without a reliable detector:

- **Do not accuse based on a detector score.** It is not defensible.
- **Have a conversation.** "Walk me through how you wrote this." A student who did the
  work can talk about it; that is not a gotcha, it is a normal instructional conversation.
- **Look at the trajectory** — a sudden discontinuity from a student's established work
  is information, but it is not proof. Students improve, and students get help.
- **Check whether the expectation was stated.** Frequently it was not.
- **Treat first instances as instructional**, consistent with SPPS's restorative practices
  framing. The district embeds digital citizenship in PBIS restorative practices — the
  AI response should match that posture, not contradict it.
- **Follow district policy**, and involve administration per that policy.

## Hands-on block

Each participant takes **one real assessment they will give this semester** and redesigns
it, applying at least two of the four strategies, and assigns traffic-light expectations
with language they will actually put on the assignment.

Then **stress-test in pairs:** the partner's job is to try to complete the redesigned
assessment using AI. If they can produce a passing product in five minutes with a single
prompt, the redesign is not finished.

This adversarial test is the most valuable part of the module. Participants consistently
believe their redesign is robust until a colleague breaks it in two minutes.

Non-teaching staff adapt: counselors and administrators redesign a student-facing
process or reflection; instructional coaches build the redesign protocol they will use
with the teachers they support.

## The artifact — a redesigned assessment

```
ASSESSMENT: [name]              COURSE: ______
WHAT IT ACTUALLY MEASURES: ______

ORIGINAL VULNERABILITY: [how a student completes this with one prompt]

STRATEGIES APPLIED: ☐ visible process ☐ higher-order/local
                    ☐ in-room component ☐ explicit AI use

EXPECTATION: 🔴 / 🟡 / 🟢
LANGUAGE ON THE ASSIGNMENT: "______"

PARTNER STRESS TEST: [what my partner produced with AI, and my response]
```

## Pitfalls

**The detection trap.** Some participants desperately want a tool that catches cheaters.
Be firm and be specific about the equity harm. Redirect to design.

**Prohibition as the whole strategy.** Red-light everything and students use AI anyway,
invisibly, without guidance. Name that outcome explicitly.

**Redesign overload.** Nobody can rebuild every assessment. Scope it: **one assessment
per unit** is a realistic year-one target. Say so, or participants will leave defeated.

**Assuming student access.** Do not design activities requiring student AI accounts
without confirming under-18 access. ⚠️ Have an adult-mediated variant ready — the
teacher runs the AI on the projector, the class critiques the output together. This
works well and requires no student accounts at all.

**Equity blind spots.** Students have unequal access to AI outside school and unequal
support in using it well. An assignment assuming home access to a paid AI tool creates a
gap. And the English-only limits in parts of the stack affect multilingual students
directly.

## Check for understanding

1. Why is AI detection software a poor foundation for academic integrity policy in this
   district specifically?
2. Take a traditional five-paragraph essay assignment. Name two changes that make it
   measure learning even when students have AI.
3. A student's work is dramatically better than usual. What is your first move?

*Expected: (3) a conversation — "walk me through your process" — not an accusation, and
first check whether expectations were stated.*

## iPad notes

- Student-visible process work — drafts, revision history, reflections — works well in
  Google Docs on iPad, where version history makes the trajectory visible without any
  detection tool. Teach staff to look at version history; most never have.
- In-room components (recorded explanations, quick video defenses) are genuinely easy on
  1:1 iPads. The device profile that complicates staff workflows is an **advantage**
  here — lean on it.
- If students will use any AI tool, test the exact workflow on a student iPad first.
